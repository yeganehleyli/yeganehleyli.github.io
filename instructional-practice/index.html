Instructional methods

Instructional method

ADDIE Model: Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, and Evaluation

What it is

A way to manage an instructional project from the first question about learner needs through testing and revision. Findings can send the designer back to an earlier phase.

The five phases

  1. Analysis: Find out who needs to do what, what gets in the way, and whether instruction can help.
  2. Design: Specify a learning goal, evidence of success, and practice that connects them.
  3. Development: Build the materials and try a small prototype with learners.
  4. Implementation: Prepare the setting and facilitators, then deliver the learning experience.
  5. Evaluation: Compare learner work with the goal and use the findings to revise the design.

Why use it

It gives a team a way to test its assumptions before investing in a full course and to judge the result by what learners can do.

Practical example

Suppose adults compare phone plans by the advertised price alone. A designer could inspect their calculations, pilot a fee-comparison exercise, then check whether learners include fees when comparing two unfamiliar plans.

Further readingRobert Maribe Branch · Instructional Design: The ADDIE ApproachWake Forest University · Instructional Design Methods

Instructional method

Backward Design: Understanding by Design (UbD)

What it is

Grant Wiggins and Jay McTighe’s framework starts with what learners should understand and use independently, then works back to evidence and instruction.

The three stages

  1. Identify the desired results: Name what learners should understand and use in a new situation.
  2. Determine acceptable evidence: Choose a performance task and decide what would count as success.
  3. Plan the learning: Sequence the examples, practice, and feedback that prepare learners for that task.

Why use it

Starting with the intended performance helps a designer spot activities that fill time without preparing learners for the assessment.

Practical example

A unit on scale could culminate in a floor plan for a 3-by-4-metre reading corner. First set criteria for accurate scale and furniture fit; use those criteria to plan the measuring and drawing practice.

Further readingJay McTighe and Grant Wiggins · The Understanding by Design Framework (ASCD)

Instructional method

Gagné’s Nine Events of Instruction

What it is

Robert Gagné’s nine events describe the support a lesson can offer, from directing attention to using a skill in a new setting. Several events can recur in one lesson.

The nine events

  1. Gain attention with a question, discrepancy, or problem learners can investigate.
  2. State the objective in terms of what learners will be able to do.
  3. Prompt recall of knowledge that will help with the new task.
  4. Present the new idea or demonstrate the skill.
  5. Guide learning with a worked example, cue, or explanation of a common error.
  6. Elicit performance: Give learners a chance to try the task.
  7. Give feedback that identifies a useful next move.
  8. Assess performance on a fresh attempt against the stated objective.
  9. Support retention and transfer by revisiting the skill later in a different context.

Why use it

The events separate seeing an explanation, practicing with help, and showing independent understanding. Each calls for a different design decision.

Practical example

Open with two bar charts that use the same numbers but look different. After guided practice reading the axes, learners could interpret a fresh chart independently and revisit the idea with a news graphic the following week.

Further readingNorthern Illinois University · Gagné’s Nine Events of Instruction

Instructional method

Merrill’s First Principles of Instruction

What it is

M. David Merrill puts a meaningful whole task at the center of instruction. Four principles describe how learners can prepare for it, see it performed, try it, and bring it into their own lives.

Design principles

  • Center instruction on a whole task that learners recognize as useful.
  • Activation: Bring relevant prior experience or knowledge to mind.
  • Demonstration: Show how to do the task and make important decisions visible.
  • Application: Have learners perform a similar task with feedback and gradually less help.
  • Integration: Invite learners to adapt, explain, or use the skill in their own setting.

Why use it

A whole task gives the smaller skills a purpose. Learners can see where each one matters and how the pieces fit together.

Practical example

A workshop could center on responding to a fraction misconception. Teachers recall a similar exchange, watch a modeled response, rehearse with a partner, and adapt the response for an upcoming lesson.

Further readingM. David Merrill · First Principles of Instruction

Instructional method

Universal Design for Learning (UDL)

What it is

CAST’s framework helps designers anticipate barriers, set clear goals, and offer learners meaningful options for engaging with ideas and showing what they know.

Design principles

  • Engagement: Make the goal clear and offer relevant choices that support participation.
  • Representation: Explain the same key relationship in more than one accessible way.
  • Action and expression: Offer appropriate ways to practice or respond, using criteria tied to the goal.

Why use it

A learner can understand a concept yet face a barrier in the format of the task. Planning options helps preserve the challenge that matters.

Practical example

If the goal is to justify an estimate, learners could choose a context, consult a described diagram or data table, and explain their reasoning in speech, writing, or a labeled sketch. All responses use the same criteria.

Further readingCAST · Universal Design for Learning Guidelines 3.0

Instructional method

Bloom’s Revised Taxonomy of Educational Objectives

What it is

The revision led by Lorin Anderson and David Krathwohl distinguishes types of knowledge and six kinds of thinking. Choose the thinking the task calls for; a lesson need not visit every category in order.

Cognitive processes

  • Remember: Ask learners to recall a relevant fact or term.
  • Understand: Ask them to explain an idea in their own words.
  • Apply: Have them use a procedure on a particular example.
  • Analyze: Ask them to identify parts and examine how they relate.
  • Evaluate: Have them judge a choice using stated criteria and evidence.
  • Create: Ask them to bring elements together into a new, coherent product.

Why use it

It helps a designer notice when an assessment asks for recall but the learning goal requires a judgment, explanation, or original product.

Practical example

To assess judgment about a claim on school attendance, a designer might show two charts and ask which supports the claim and why. Naming each type of chart would assess a different objective.

Further readingDavid R. Krathwohl · A Revision of Bloom’s Taxonomy: An Overview

Instructional strategies

Instructional strategy

Microlearning

What it is

A short, self-contained lesson teaches one action or idea and gives the learner a chance to use it.

How to use it

  1. Choose one decision or skill small enough to practice in one sitting.
  2. Show one clear example and ask for a fresh attempt.
  3. Give feedback and point to the next task where the skill matters.

Why use it

A narrow target makes room for practice even when time is short. A later task can reconnect that small skill to the larger goal.

Practical example

A short grocery-label lesson could model the price per can in a six-pack costing $9. Learners then calculate the price per can in an eight-pack costing $10 and choose the better buy.

Further readingNC State University · Micro-Learning with Digital Tools

Instructional strategy

Guest experts and resource speakers

What it is

A guest practitioner brings a real decision, artifact, or perspective into a lesson so learners can examine how someone uses the subject outside class.

How to use it

  1. Brief the guest on the learning goal and request a concrete case.
  2. Help learners prepare questions about the guest’s decisions.
  3. Debrief the visit by applying one idea to a new example.

Why use it

Questions about a specific decision can reveal judgments and tradeoffs that a textbook description leaves out.

Practical example

A surveyor could bring a site plan and explain why one measurement needed checking. Learners might question that choice, then mark which dimension they would verify on a different plan.

Further readingJohns Hopkins University · Guest Speakers in the Classroom

Instructional strategy

Blended learning

What it is

Online and face-to-face activities deliberately build on each other within one learning experience.

How to use it

  1. Choose an outcome shared by both settings.
  2. Assign a task to each setting for a clear reason.
  3. Carry work from the first task into the second and provide feedback.

Why use it

Independent time can support preparation or rehearsal; meeting together gives learners a place to test and discuss what they brought.

Practical example

Before class, learners could annotate a misleading graph online. In the room, pairs compare annotations and jointly redraw the graph so it tells a more accurate story.

Further readingLouisiana State University · Designing Blended Courses

Instructional strategy

Instructor-led training (ILT)

What it is

An instructor guides a group through a skill in real time, in person or online, and can adjust the explanation in response to learners.

How to use it

  1. Set a clear performance target and model a short example.
  2. Pause for everyone to attempt a similar task.
  3. Inspect responses and use a common difficulty to shape the next example.

Why use it

A facilitator can hear how learners reach an answer and address a misunderstanding while the decision is still fresh.

Practical example

An instructor could model how to read a bus timetable, then ask everyone to plan an arrival by 8:30. Confusing departure and arrival columns would be a cue to model that step again.

Further readingUniversity of Iowa · Engaging and Interactive Lecture Strategies

Instructional strategy

Gamification

What it is

A designer adds selected game elements, such as missions, progress markers, or retries, to a learning task.

How to use it

  1. Name the behavior the activity should reward.
  2. Choose a game element that makes that behavior visible.
  3. Allow retries with helpful feedback and check the underlying work.

Why use it

A well-chosen progress marker can show what a learner has tried and what remains to practice. Rewarding speed would send a different message from rewarding reasoning.

Practical example

In a ‘graph detective’ activity, a learner could earn a clue by identifying a misleading axis and explaining its effect. The next clue unlocks after a reasoned answer or a successful revision.

Further readingUniversity of Waterloo · Gamification and Game-Based Learning

Instructional strategy

Social and peer learning

What it is

Learners develop an idea by explaining their reasoning, questioning one another, and revising their work together.

How to use it

  1. Pose a question worth discussing and allow individual thinking time.
  2. Ask partners to compare explanations and question one assumption.
  3. Have each learner revise an answer and name what changed.

Why use it

Comparing explanations makes reasoning visible. An individual revision shows whether each learner can use what the group discussed.

Practical example

A pair could compare 20% off $50 with a $12 coupon. One learner reasons in tens and the other multiplies; each explains the other’s method before choosing the larger saving.

Further readingCornell University · Short Peer Discussion Activities

Instructional strategy

Interactive webinars

What it is

A live online seminar connects a presenter and a distributed audience around a focused question or demonstration.

How to use it

  1. Share the guiding question and accessible materials before the session.
  2. Break the presentation for a prediction, poll, or short response.
  3. Use responses during the session and share an accessible recap afterward.

Why use it

A live poll lets the presenter see a pattern across a dispersed group and use it to decide what to explain next.

Practical example

In a webinar, teachers could vote on which of two exit tickets reveals student reasoning. The presenter shows sample responses, then invites teachers to revise one question in the chat.

Further readingUniversity of Bristol · Synchronous Online Sessions

Instructional strategy

Hands-on training

What it is

Learners perform the target action with tools or materials, inspect the result, and adjust what they do.

How to use it

  1. Introduce the materials and show what a successful result looks like.
  2. Let learners build, measure, or manipulate something themselves.
  3. Ask them to compare the result with the goal and explain one adjustment.

Why use it

A visible product gives learners and instructors something specific to inspect. It can reveal a misunderstanding that a correct answer alone would hide.

Practical example

Learners could mark three quarters on a paper number line, then align it with a same-length line divided into eighths. Matching the marks gives them a way to explain why six eighths is the same amount.

Further readingInstitute of Education Sciences · Fractions on a Number Line

Instructional strategy

Spaced practice and repetition

What it is

Learners return to a skill in sessions separated by time. A new attempt can start with retrieval before the learner consults an example.

How to use it

  1. Identify something learners will need to recall beyond today.
  2. Schedule brief return visits with gaps between them.
  3. Vary the examples, give feedback, and adjust the next gap if needed.

Why use it

Returning after a delay can support longer-term recall. The useful interval depends in part on when learners will need the knowledge.

Practical example

After a lesson on median, learners could find the median of a new data set the next day, the following week, and later in the term. Each time they try before reviewing the worked solution.

Further readingNicholas J. Cepeda and colleagues · Spacing Effects in LearningInstitute of Education Sciences · Organizing Instruction and Study

Instructional strategy

Audio learning

What it is

Spoken explanation, dialogue, or a recorded think-aloud becomes material for learners to listen to, pause, and examine.

How to use it

  1. Choose a task where hearing language or reasoning serves the goal.
  2. Record a focused clip, add pause points, and provide an accurate transcript.
  3. Ask learners to identify a decision or try the reasoning themselves.

Why use it

Speech can make phrasing and the order of a decision audible. A transcript makes the same material available for reading and review.

Practical example

A short recording could model how a reader notices that ‘they’ has two possible referents. Learners pause, inspect the sentence in the transcript, and rewrite it to remove the ambiguity.

Further readingW3C Web Accessibility Initiative · Making Audio and Video Media Accessible

Instructional strategy

Storytelling

What it is

A short narrative gives a concept a setting, a problem, and a consequence that learners can interpret.

How to use it

  1. Build a brief story around one decision and the concept it illustrates.
  2. Pause before the outcome and ask learners to predict what happens.
  3. Reveal the outcome and ask which detail explains it.

Why use it

A sequence of events makes it possible to ask when an assumption stopped being true and what changed because of it.

Practical example

A café owner lowers a $20 lunch price by 20%, then raises the new price by 20%. Ask learners to predict the ending before revealing $19.20 and naming the base used for each change.

Further readingMelanie C. Green · Storytelling in Teaching

Instructional strategy

Online simulations

What it is

Learners interact with a model of a system, change selected inputs, and observe what follows under the model’s rules.

How to use it

  1. Explain what the model represents and what it leaves out.
  2. Ask for a prediction, then change one input and run a trial.
  3. Compare runs and discuss which conclusions the model can support.

Why use it

Repeatable trials let learners test a conjecture and inspect variation while holding other conditions steady.

Practical example

On a virtual pegboard, learners could predict where balls will land, increase the chance of a bounce to the right, and compare the resulting distributions across several runs.

Further readingPhET Interactive Simulations · Plinko Probability

Instructional strategy

Just-in-time training (JITT)

What it is

A short explanation or job aid is available at the moment someone needs to complete a particular task.

How to use it

  1. Identify the moment someone stops, hesitates, or looks for help.
  2. Write the smallest useful set of steps or one worked example.
  3. Put it where the task happens and test whether someone can use it unaided.

Why use it

An aid placed beside a task can supply a seldom-used detail without requiring the learner to remember an entire procedure.

Practical example

Beside a spreadsheet’s ‘percentage change’ field, a help link could show how to subtract the old value from the new one and divide by the old value, with a worked example from 40 to 50.

Further readingEffective Job Aids · Checklist for Creating Effective Job Aids

Instructional strategy

Problem-solving

What it is

Learners work toward a defined goal when the route is not supplied. They choose a representation or strategy, test it, and justify their answer.

How to use it

  1. Set a problem with a clear goal and meaningful constraints.
  2. Ask learners to state what they know and choose an approach.
  3. Have them check the result and compare it with another possible approach.

Why use it

The explanation reveals how learners choose and test a method, including whether their answer makes sense in context.

Practical example

For 28 guests, learners could compare six-roll packs at $4 and ten-roll packs at $6. They would choose a combination and defend it in terms of both cost and leftovers.

Further readingUniversity of Waterloo · Teaching Problem-Solving Skills

Instructional strategy

Practice drills

What it is

A brief series of similar attempts rehearses a skill that learners have already been taught, with a chance to correct errors.

How to use it

  1. Model the strategy first and check that learners can explain it.
  2. Offer a small set of varied prompts and give prompt feedback.
  3. Return to errors, then check the skill inside a meaningful task.

Why use it

Short, focused repetition can build fluency and show which parts still need explanation. Accuracy and strategy use give more information than speed alone.

Practical example

After modeling ‘make ten,’ a teacher could use 8 + 7, 9 + 6, and 8 + 5. A missed item prompts a demonstration with counters before another try.

Further readingNational Center on Intensive Intervention · Basic Facts

Instructional strategy

Scenario-based learning

What it is

Learners take a role in a plausible situation, make a decision, and examine a consequence. The next situation may change with their choice.

How to use it

  1. Define the learner’s role, the decision, and what is at stake.
  2. Write credible alternatives and a plausible response to each.
  3. Ask for a justification, show the consequence, and allow a revision.

Why use it

A scenario lets people rehearse judgment when several responses sound reasonable and discuss which detail should guide the decision.

Practical example

A child says one eighth is greater than one sixth because eight is larger than six. Teachers could choose a follow-up question, read the child’s reply, and revise their next move.

Further readingVanderbilt University · Branching Out with Interactive Scenarios

Instructional strategy

Personalized and self-guided learning

What it is

A pathway can be personalized around a learner’s starting point or goals. In self-guided learning, the learner helps choose the route and monitor progress.

How to use it

  1. Offer a starting check and agree on a goal and success criteria.
  2. Provide a manageable choice of tasks or levels of support.
  3. Build in a progress check and a clear way to ask for help or change route.

Why use it

Meaningful choices let learners spend time on a specific need. A shared destination and checkpoints give the choice a purpose.

Practical example

A learner who reads graph scales accurately but struggles to describe trends could skip the axis refresher, choose a trend task, and use a rubric to check the explanation.

Further readingUniversity of Waterloo · Self-Directed Learning: A Four-Step Process

Instructional strategy

Experiential learning and evaluation

What it is

Learners try something, reflect on what happened, draw a useful idea from the evidence, and test that idea in another attempt.

How to use it

  1. Set up a concrete experience and name the evidence to collect.
  2. Compare the result with the goal and describe what shaped it.
  3. Form a principle or revised plan, then test and evaluate it in a new attempt.

Why use it

Reflection connects an event to a possible explanation. A second attempt gives learners evidence about whether their proposed change helped.

Practical example

One tutor could give directions for drawing a polygon without naming it. Comparing a partner’s drawing with the original reveals an ambiguous phrase; the tutor revises it and tries with another partner.

Further readingInstitute for Experiential Learning · What Is Experiential Learning?

Instructional strategy

Multimedia learning

What it is

Words and relevant visuals work together to explain an idea, such as a diagram with labels or an animation with narration.

How to use it

  1. Choose the relationship or process the learner needs to see.
  2. Pair each important visual change with a concise explanation.
  3. Remove distractions, provide accessible equivalents, and check understanding with a fresh example.

Why use it

A well-matched picture and explanation let learners connect a verbal idea to the relationship it describes. Extra media need a reason to be there.

Practical example

An animation could fill a rectangle with four rows of six tiles as narration connects each row to 4 × 6. A still diagram and text description keep the same relationship available for review.
