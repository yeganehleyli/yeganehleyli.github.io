Research that reshapes teaching

Examining Shifts in Secondary STEM Teachers’ Understanding of Engineering Through a Microelectronics Research Experience for Teachers (RET) Program

Examining how authentic microelectronics research experiences relate to teachers’ understanding of engineering and engineering research.

My contribution
Program administrator, researcher, and data analyst
Participants
8 middle and high school teachers
Project context
NSF-funded RET program at Binghamton University’s Watson College

In this case study

The professional-learning challengeHow I contributedWhat the responses showedWhat I learned

Tools & methods

Pre/post questionnaires, a five-category coding framework, independent coding, descriptive statistics, and written-response analysis.

The professional-learning challenge

Authentic research can give teachers a more concrete view of engineering, but evaluating that experience requires looking beyond participation. During a six-week NSF-funded RET program, I helped coordinate research experiences in microelectronics and semiconductor manufacturing and examined shifts in teachers’ understanding of engineering practices.

The study focused on eight middle and high school teachers and their explanations before and after the program.

How I contributed

  1. Coordinate across disciplines

    As part of an interdisciplinary Binghamton University team, I supported the program and its research activities, connecting professional learning with authentic engineering contexts.

  2. Code understanding consistently

    We analyzed pre- and post-program questionnaires covering 11 constructs. The five-category framework ranged from 0, “uncategorized,” to 4, “fully informed.” Two researchers coded responses independently and resolved differences through discussion.

  3. Read scores alongside explanations

    I contributed to descriptive analysis and examined changes in teachers’ language, specificity, and examples drawn from laboratory and industry experiences.

What the responses showed

10 of 11

Teachers’ written explanations shifted from broad descriptions toward more specific accounts grounded in laboratory activities, industry experiences, and observations of practicing engineers. Their responses offered richer descriptions of collaboration, creativity, design constraints, and failure.

The lower empirical-testing score identified a dimension that warranted further investigation rather than a uniformly positive interpretation of the program.

How to read the evidence

These are descriptive findings from eight teachers. The design does not establish causality, generalizability, or subsequent classroom impact.

What I learned

Scores and written explanations answer different questions. Reading them together helped me understand both the direction of change and how participants described their developing knowledge.

The uneven results reinforced the importance of investigating exceptions. In future evaluations, I would add follow-up interviews and classroom evidence to examine whether teachers retain these understandings and translate them into instruction.
