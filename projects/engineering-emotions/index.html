Making room for emotion

Exploring Elementary Students’ Emotional States within Engineering Design Tasks in an Afterschool Program (Fundamental)

Designing hands-on engineering activities while listening to how children experience success, difficulty, and frustration.

My contribution
STEM instructor, technology tutor, researcher, and first author
Participants
9 learners in grades 3–5
Project context
NSF-supported afterschool program at a New York public library

In this case study

The learning challengeHow I designed and supported the experienceWhat we foundWhat I learned

Tools & methods

Makey-Makey, robots, drones, light-up cards, visual emotion tracking, and structured interviews.

The learning challenge

Engineering activities invite children to experiment, but unfamiliar parts and unsuccessful designs can also create frustration. In an NSF-supported afterschool program with a Binghamton University team, I designed and taught activities while investigating how task design and instructional support shaped students’ emotional experiences.

The aim was to understand those experiences and use them to inform emotionally supportive STEM instruction.

How I designed and supported the experience

  1. Make the outcome tangible

    I facilitated Makey-Makey pianos, robot building, drone assembly, light-up cards, and toy modification. These activities gave learners opportunities to create something they could see, use, and share.

  2. Support the difficult moments

    I helped students troubleshoot, broke down demanding tasks, and created opportunities for peer collaboration. Support responded to the challenges students encountered while building.

  3. Make room for emotional feedback

    Our team used visual emotion-tracking sheets and structured interviews with nine learners. I worked with collaborators to categorize positive and negative emotions and examine patterns across creative and technically demanding activities.

What we found

88%

of reported emotional responses were positive; 12% were negative.

Positive responses included excitement, happiness, interest, and pride. Negative responses included frustration and confusion. These percentages describe emotional responses, not the percentage of students who improved.

  • Visible, functional outcomes—such as playing a piano or illuminating a card—were often associated with excitement and pride.
  • Drone assembly and toy modification could prompt frustration when components were unfamiliar or designs did not work.
  • Students described asking peers for help, trying alternatives, and feeling proud when a project eventually worked.

The work resulted in a first-author 2025 ASEE paper and practical insights for balancing achievable successes, meaningful challenges, and timely instructional support.

How to read the evidence

This was an exploratory study of a small, self-selected group in one program. It did not directly measure gains in achievement, engagement, or resilience.

What I learned

I learned to treat emotions as instructional feedback. Frustration could indicate a need for clearer guidance, smaller steps, or peer support; excitement often accompanied visible progress and creative ownership.

This experience strengthened my ability to connect hands-on instruction with research. In future work, I would combine emotion tracking and interviews with performance assessments and longer-term follow-up to examine relationships with learning and persistence.
