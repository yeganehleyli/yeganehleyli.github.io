Stories into sequences

Sequencing Nursery Rhymes Through Early Coding in Preschool Settings

Connecting familiar stories with hands-on, screen-free coding to support sequencing in a small preschool group.

My contribution
Coauthor; documenting and communicating the instructional approach
Participants
4 children, ages 4 and 5
Project context
Three lessons in a preschool setting; coauthored article in The Reading Teacher

In this case study

The learning challengeHow the experience was designedWhat the assessments showedWhat I learned

Tools & methods

Jack and Jill, physical grids, directional arrows, story-picture cards, paired practice, pre/post assessments, and descriptive observations.

The learning challenge

I collaborated on a preschool project connecting early literacy with foundational coding concepts. Four children, ages four and five, had difficulty sequencing familiar stories. We explored how hands-on, screen-free coding could support story sequencing while introducing basic coding vocabulary.

As a coauthor, I contributed to documenting and communicating the instructional approach and its findings.

How the experience was designed

  1. Start with a familiar story

    The nursery rhyme Jack and Jill provided a shared narrative. Grids, directional arrows, and four story-picture cards connected events in the story with physical movement and early coding concepts.

  2. Model, guide, and practice together

    Across three lessons, the teacher used modeling, guided practice, paired activities, repeated vocabulary, prompting, and corrective feedback. Children practiced arranging events and using the materials.

  3. Look at sequencing and vocabulary separately

    We examined pre- and post-assessment scores alongside descriptive notes about children’s vocabulary, use of materials, and need for teacher prompts.

Calculated from the reported individual scores, mean sequencing accuracy increased by 75 percentage points, from 25% to 100%. The number of children who sequenced the complete story increased from zero to four.

  • Using the materials: two children used them without prompting, while two needed teacher prompts, as recorded in the assessment table.
  • Recalling coding vocabulary: one child recalled “arrow,” another recalled “forward,” and the other two did not recall the coding terms.
  • Connecting action and language: children commonly called the forward arrow “up,” suggesting that practical understanding developed before consistent use of the intended vocabulary.

The project resulted in a coauthored article in The Reading Teacher.

How to read the evidence

These promising observations describe four children in a small project. Without a comparison group or longer-term assessment, the findings do not establish broader effectiveness or retention.

What I learned

Familiar stories and simple physical materials can provide an accessible starting point for integrating literacy and STEM. Strong sequencing performance alongside emerging vocabulary reinforced the importance of assessing different skills separately.

Combining numerical scores with descriptive observations helped identify the need for continued modeling and vocabulary practice. In future work, I would examine retention and transfer to unfamiliar stories while documenting how much prompting each child needs.
